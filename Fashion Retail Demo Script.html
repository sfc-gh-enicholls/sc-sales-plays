Demo Script · Fashion Retail
Signal to Shelf Conceptual Demo Flow

Vertical fashion retail — one trend event, followed end to end across four planning personas — EMEA Fashion & Apparel

What we’re doing & how it relates to the play

“Stop finding out a style is dying on the shelf the same week you're forced into a deep markdown.”

This demo is the conceptual centrepiece for the “Signal to Shelf” Fashion Retail play. The play’s premise: across EMEA fashion retail, every major trading theme — markdown pressure, terminal stock, trend-chasing, and CSRD/ESPR compliance — is fundamentally a data problem. Fragmented systems force buying and merchandising teams to react to sell-through after the fact; Snowflake collapses that into one governed platform so they see it live and act early.

Positioning: Signal to Shelf helps EMEA fashion retail leaders turn fragmented trading data into a single, trusted intelligence layer — so every decision, from chase to markdown to compliance, is made with days of runway, not hindsight.

What this demo proves: a single trend event — skinny jeans losing share to flare jeans mid-season — followed through every consequence it triggers, across four personas, all on Snowflake. It maps to the play’s arc: Signal (Cortex surfaces the trend before the planner goes looking — S1) → Shelf (the response propagates through MFP, assortment, OTB, forecast, markdown, replenishment and supply — S2–5, and forward into pre-season — S6) → Proof (S4c: supplier compliance is a live constraint on the chase, evidenced from the same governed data — not a separate reporting exercise).

A note on positioning the planning surfaces. This demo shows connected planning built natively on Snowflake — MFP, assortment, OTB, replenishment and supplier collaboration all computing over one governed data foundation. Position it as what a connected planning layer looks like when every horizon shares one set of data. It is deliberately not a claim that Snowflake replaces the customer’s planning suite; where a suite is already in place, the same story lands as the intelligence and data layer beneath it that the suite cannot provide on its own.

Fictional Company — Demo Context
Company
Calla
Premium contemporary vertical fashion retailer (COS / Arket tier). Designs, develops and sells its own garments across ~400 global stores and a growing DTC e-commerce channel.
Trading Model
Because Calla controls the full product lifecycle from design to shop floor it has more levers than a wholesale buyer — but it also carries the stock risk. Supplier lead times stretch 9–12 months.
Planning Context
Three horizons managed simultaneously: in-season reaction, pre-season buying commitment, and long-lead supplier capacity. Trend windows are 3–4 weeks.

The Narrative Event — One Trend Shift, Four Consequences

The signal: Flare jeans — historically a minor SKU in Calla’s denim assortment — start selling at 2.4x the seasonal baseline. Skinny jeans, the core volume driver, fall away.
Markdown exposure: Skinny is now tracking 34% below plan with committed inventory and open replenishment orders still landing — terminal stock unless it is cleared early and shallow.
Chase opportunity: Flare is 67% above plan and stocking out across the London, Amsterdam and Stockholm cluster. Capturing it means new supply inside the trend window.
Compliance constraint: The primary flare vendor has no spare capacity. The alternatives that can deliver in time are the ones Calla has vetted least — and any garment they make carries CSDDD due-diligence, ESPR/Digital Product Passport and restricted-substance obligations into 400 stores.

Section 1 — Connected Planning & the Trend Signal

Persona: Category Planner
Scene 1a · Connected Retail Planning Homepage
Setup
The Category Planner starts their day in Calla’s planning homepage — a single connected view across merchandise financial planning (MFP), assortment planning, and supply chain planning. Every KPI is computed by Dynamic Tables refreshing incrementally as overnight store sales, DC movements and web orders land.
What to show
  • Connected planning homepage in Streamlit in Snowflake: MFP actuals vs. plan, assortment performance by class, and supply chain health (stock coverage, in-transit, open POs) — all querying Snowflake live
  • No switching between systems — the same data that feeds finance feeds the planner’s view, eliminating the reconciliation that historically consumed Monday mornings
  • Role-based layout: the Category Planner sees the denim category; a different role sees a different scope — governed by row-level security, not by three separate logins
Aha moment
Call out: One application, one data platform. MFP, assortment and supply chain have historically lived in separate tools at most retailers. Here the planner doesn’t open three systems to understand their category.
So what
Outcome: Complete situational awareness at the start of every planning day. For planners managing global line plans across hundreds of SKUs, this is the difference between spending Monday on reporting and spending it on decisions.
Scene 1b · AI Insight — The Flare Jeans Trend Signal
Setup
The planner hasn’t gone looking for this. Cortex has surfaced it — and has already cross-referenced POS sell-through acceleration, search and social trend data from the Snowflake Marketplace, and the store-level stockout pattern, all joined without any ETL.
What to show
  • Cortex ML anomaly detection flags flare jeans as an outlier: sell-through in the last three weeks running 2.4x the seasonal baseline, surfaced as a Snowflake Alert on the planner’s homepage
  • A simultaneous supply-side signal: store-level stockout network view showing flare stockouts spreading across the London, Amsterdam and Stockholm cluster — fed by live DC allocation data
  • Third-party trend data from the Snowflake Marketplace joined directly to Calla’s item-level sell-through — Cortex has already correlated the external trend lift with the in-store stockout pattern and written a plain-English insight card
Aha moment
Call out: The insight is not a report the planner ran. Anomaly detection spotted the signal, enriched it with external trend data from the Marketplace, and pushed it as an actionable alert. The planner didn’t go looking — the platform came to them.
So what
Outcome: In vertical fashion the trend window is short. This is a two-to-three week advantage over competitors still running weekly reporting cycles — and that window is the difference between capturing a trend and missing it.
Scene 1c · Spin Up a Scenario — Flare Jeans Demand Response
Setup
The planner spins up a planning scenario in place, to model Calla’s response to the flare phenomenon.
What to show
  • Scenario branches off the current base plan — the base plan is the source, and the branch recomputes impacted metrics (revenue, margin, cover, OTB) incrementally as parameters change
  • Scenario named “Flare Jean Trend Response Q2”, stored as a first-class object, versioned, and immediately visible to every downstream persona — no export, no share, no copy
  • Snowflake Tasks notify the demand planner and inventory planner that a scenario is in progress — the handoff is in the platform, not in someone’s inbox
Aha moment
Call out: Scenario branching doesn’t copy a spreadsheet — it creates an incremental computation branch. Every downstream plan that references the scenario (OTB, assortment, replenishment) recomputes automatically when its parameters change.
So what
Outcome: Multi-persona planning on a single scenario, in real time, without version-control risk. This is the operating model most retailers are still trying to reach by emailing Excel files.

Section 2 — Merchant Command Centre

Persona: Category Planner (cont.)
Scene 2a · Underperforming vs. Overperforming Items
Setup
The planner moves to the Merchant Command Centre — the item-level performance view — to confirm with transaction data what the AI insight signalled.
What to show
  • Item performance view: skinny jeans tracking 34% below the target sales plan; flare 67% above — both against live POS actuals streamed from store tills and the e-commerce platform
  • Cortex Analyst takes the question in plain English — “Show me the last four weeks of sell-through for skinny vs. flare jeans across all regions” — and answers it from the item and store tables in seconds
  • Crossover chart: the exact point at which flare overtook skinny in units sold, computed automatically, no manual pivot
Aha moment
Call out: A business question in plain English, answered from live data. No analyst request, no overnight query, no spreadsheet export.
So what
Outcome: The trend signal is confirmed with actual transaction data. The planner is now confident in the scenario — and has the evidence to justify the decisions to their Merchandising Director.
Scene 2b · MFP Update — In-Season vs. Actual
Setup
The planner updates the Merchandise Financial Plan to reflect the new reality: uplift the flare sales plan, reduce skinny.
What to show
  • MFP in-season vs. actual: current plan against actuals side by side, with variance flagged by anomaly detection rather than eyeballed
  • The planner adjusts flare up and skinny down within the scenario — the MFP recomputes revenue, margin and sell-through rate incrementally across every remaining week of the season
  • The update propagates: the OTB calculation and assortment coverage metrics move in the same computation chain, and the knock-on effect is visible in the same view
Aha moment
Call out: MFP changes propagate downstream automatically. The planner doesn’t manually update OTB and assortment after adjusting the financial plan — every dependent metric recomputes.
So what
Outcome: The “connected plan” retailers have chased for years. One change in MFP ripples correctly through OTB and assortment — no broken spreadsheet formula, no overnight batch.
Scene 2c · Assortment Update — Shift 20% from Skinny to Flare
Setup
The planner moves 20% of the denim slot allocation from skinny to flare — and critically, this needs to flow through to future seasons, not just patch in-season.
What to show
  • Cluster-level assortment grid: current denim slot allocation by store cluster (fashion-forward urban vs. suburban mainstream)
  • The planner reallocates 20% across the relevant clusters — assortment cover, option count and space productivity recompute instantly
  • Multi-season propagation: because assortment is modelled as a computation chain, the reallocation flows automatically through Q3, Q4 and into pre-season — the planner toggles forward and sees the impact on future seasons without opening another system
  • Cluster variation driven by Cortex ML segmentation on historical style preference: London and Stockholm take a larger flare allocation than suburban clusters
Aha moment
Call out: The assortment change reaches pre-season with no manual update in a separate planning horizon. This is the “black hole” between in-season and pre-season, closed.
So what
Outcome: Calla isn’t just fixing today — it’s shaping next season. Competitors working in disconnected tools will place their pre-season buys without knowing what in-season data is telling them.
Scene 2d · OTB — Scenario Analysis
Setup
The planner now needs to reallocate Open-to-Buy to fund the increased flare receipts, taking budget from a lower-priority category that still has unspent OTB.
What to show
  • OTB scenario view: remaining OTB by category with planned vs. actual receipts and end-of-period inventory
  • The planner increases OTB for the jeans class and reduces it for a category with remaining budget and lower urgency (e.g. jersey tops) — the OTB balance and receipt plan recompute immediately
  • OTB extends into pre-season: the position is visible all the way forward, not just for the current month
  • Cortex Analyst: “Which categories have the most uncommitted OTB remaining this season?” — answered straight from the financial tables
Aha moment
Call out: OTB is usually a monthly meeting run in spreadsheets. Here it’s a live query — every category’s position at any moment, and reallocations modelled in real time with downstream impact shown.
So what
Outcome: MFP, assortment and OTB adjusted in one connected scenario. The handoff to demand and inventory planning carries no reconciliation risk — everyone is on the same data.

Section 3 — Demand Response & Markdown

Persona: Demand Planner
Scene 3a · Analytics & AI Forecast Impact
Setup
The demand planner picks up the Task-triggered notification that the scenario is ready for demand input, and opens the analytics view to see how the trend shift has broken the existing forecasts.
What to show
  • Cortex ML forecast vs. actuals for skinny and flare, showing exactly where the statistical model has diverged from the trend
  • Model selection in the UI: the planner shifts both styles from a standard seasonal model to a higher-reactivity trend-following model — a configuration change, not a project
  • The re-forecast computes in place, and the updated weekly demand curve appears side by side with current inventory cover
Aha moment
Call out: No rebuilding a model, no offline script. The planner changes the algorithm setting and Cortex ML retrains and re-forecasts in the platform — and the result is instantly visible to the category and inventory planners. No file exchange.
So what
Outcome: Demand model changes propagate across the planning team immediately. There is one forecast, and everyone is working off it.
Scene 3b · Activate a Promotion on Skinny Jeans
Setup
Too much skinny inventory against a weakening trend. The demand planner needs a promotion to accelerate sell-through and free up space and OTB for flare — early and shallow, before it becomes late and deep.
What to show
  • Promotion planning: the planner selects skinny, models a markdown, and sees the demand uplift estimate from Cortex ML price elasticity modelling
  • The promotion scenario returns expected sell-through acceleration, revised end-of-period inventory and margin impact — all recomputed in the same chain
  • Cortex suggests depth and duration from Calla’s own historical promotion response — e.g. “a 25% markdown in Week 14 is projected to clear 68% of remaining stock within two weeks” — with the resulting gross margin shown alongside
  • The decision triggers a Task notifying the inventory planner and DC team that skinny is entering a promo period, so open POs can be reviewed before they land
Aha moment
Call out: The recommendation isn’t a planner’s intuition. Cortex is reading Calla’s full historical promotion response and applying elasticity modelling to produce an evidence-based depth and timing.
So what
Outcome: Promotions right-sized from the start — not so deep that margin is destroyed, not so shallow that stock doesn’t clear. And the downstream teams know the moment it is activated.

Section 4 — Inventory, Supply & Supplier Compliance

Persona: Inventory Planner
Scene 4a · Replenishment Planning — Skinny Jeans in Promo
Setup
The inventory planner is notified that skinny is entering a promotion period, and opens replenishment planning to review alerts and adjust ordering behaviour before stock lands into a markdown.
What to show
  • Alerts flagging skinny replenishment orders scheduled before the promo was activated — now creating overstock risk
  • The planner works the alert queue: which orders to suppress, which to reduce, given promo-driven sell-through acceleration
  • Cortex Analyst: “Show me all open replenishment orders for skinny jeans in the next four weeks and their expected stock cover post-promo” — answered from the inventory and order tables in seconds
Aha moment
Call out: The promotion activation itself triggered the replenishment review. The inventory planner didn’t have to monitor the demand planner’s actions separately — the platform connected the events.
So what
Outcome: Avoids the classic retail error of replenishing into a promotion and creating a post-promo overstock problem out of the markdown that was meant to fix it.
Scene 4b · DC Inventory Planning — Firm vs. Cancellable POs
Setup
The planner reviews the wider DC position for skinny — specifically, which supplier purchase orders can still be cancelled or reduced, and which are already firm.
What to show
  • Open PO list for skinny with lead-time status: which POs sit inside the supplier cut-off window (firm) and which can still be cancelled or quantity-reduced
  • Cortex segments the list automatically: “the next five POs are firm; POs 6–12 can be modified” — with new requirements already calculated from the revised forecast and promo sell-through
  • The requirement calculation runs in the chain: revised demand + promo sell-through projection − current inventory = updated order requirement by week
  • Supplier lead-time data refreshes directly from EDI feeds — no manually maintained lead-time table going stale in a corner
Aha moment
Call out: Firm vs. cancellable is computed from live lead-time data — no manual calendar check, no email to procurement. The planner sees exactly what they can change, with the revised quantities already calculated.
So what
Outcome: Calla stops landing inventory it can’t sell. In vertical fashion, where the retailer owns the stock risk, cancelling the right POs at the right time is directly accretive to margin.
Scene 4c · Flare Chase — Segment Change, Supplier Compliance & Collaboration PROOF
Setup
Flare is outperforming and supply has to increase fast. Cortex ML has already reclassified the product from a B-Y segment (moderate volume, slow replenishment) to A-X (high volume, fast replenishment), which changes how it is replenished and allocated. But the primary vendor has no spare capacity inside the trend window — so the planner has to bring alternative supply online in weeks. That is the moment compliance stops being a reporting exercise and becomes a constraint on a revenue decision.
The tension
Say this out loud: the vendors who can turn a chase order fastest are usually the ones Calla has vetted least. Under CSDDD due-diligence duties, the ESPR Digital Product Passport regime, and restricted-substance obligations, a garment made in an unvetted facility — or by an undisclosed subcontractor of a vetted one — is a liability Calla puts into 400 stores at its own risk. The chase decision and the compliance decision are the same decision, made in the same week, by the same person.
What to show
  • Segment reclassification: flare moves B-Y → A-X automatically, as Cortex ML re-runs the ABC/XYZ segmentation on updated sell-through — and lead time for flare production is shown directly from the supply table, so the planner knows how fast Calla can respond
  • The sourcing question, asked honestly. Cortex Analyst: “Which of our active suppliers for woven bottoms has available capacity in the next six weeks — and of those, which have a current social audit, no unapproved subcontracting flags, and material composition data complete enough for a Digital Product Passport?” One question, answered against capacity and compliance in the same query, because they live in the same place
  • Vendor scorecard with compliance alongside commercials: OTIF and capacity next to audit currency and grade, subcontractor disclosure status, restricted-substance (REACH / ZDHC) certificates, and traceability depth — tier 1, tier 2, or back to fibre
  • The trade-off surfaces before the order, not after the shipment. The fastest-responding vendor has capacity but an audit expiring in three weeks and only tier-1 traceability. Flagged automatically as a condition on the chase, with the remediation cost and the delay cost of the compliant alternative quantified side by side
  • Compliance evidence is data, not a filing cabinet. Audit reports, test certificates and supplier declarations arrive as documents — AI_EXTRACT and Cortex Search parse them and join them to the same supplier keys as capacity and OTIF. One supplier record carries both the commercial and the compliance truth, and Snowflake’s governance (tagging, masking, lineage) lets the buyer and the sustainability team work from it without either team maintaining a private copy
  • Collaboration carries the obligation with the order. Snowflake Data Sharing publishes the revised demand signal and order requirement straight into the chosen vendor’s own Snowflake account — and the same share defines the DPP and traceability fields the vendor must return against that order. No email attachment, no portal upload, no chasing evidence six months later at reporting time
Aha moment
Call out: Compliance isn’t a workflow running weeks behind the buy — it’s a predicate in the sourcing query. Capacity without compliance was never available capacity; it just looked like it, because the two sat in different systems. And because the evidence is captured at the point of the order, CSRD and ESPR reporting later is a query against the commercial data rather than a retrospective data-collection project.
So what
Outcome: Calla captures the trend without buying a compliance liability — speed to supply and defensible sourcing, from one decision. Data Sharing removes the communication lag that costs sales when a trend window is three or four weeks, and the traceability that regulation will ask for is a by-product of how the order was placed, not a project someone funds in Q4.
If challenged
Handling “our compliance team owns that”: agree — and that’s the point. Today the compliance team owns evidence the buying team can’t see at the moment of decision, and the buying team commits volume the compliance team learns about afterwards. Neither team is doing anything wrong; the data is in two places. Put it in one and the gate closes before the order, not after the shipment.

Section 5 — Plan Review & Approval

Persona: Senior Merchandiser
Scene 5a · Review Scenarios, Approve the New Plan
Setup
The Senior Merchandiser reviews the full scenario built across the category, demand and inventory planners — including the sourcing condition attached to the flare chase — all in one place.
What to show
  • Senior review dashboard: MFP targets vs. revised plan across the scenario — revenue, margin, sell-through rate, inventory cover — queried live
  • Scenario comparison: original plan vs. “Flare Jean Trend Response Q2”, side by side
  • The sourcing decision surfaces here too: which vendor the chase is placed with, and on what compliance condition — so the approval is a commercial and a governance sign-off in one gate
  • Cortex Analyst for exec-level Q&A: “What is the total margin impact of approving this scenario vs. holding the current plan?” — answered in seconds, no analyst prep
  • One-click approval promotes the scenario to the active plan, updates every downstream computation (OTB, assortment, replenishment), and confirms out to all relevant planners
Aha moment
Call out: The approval isn’t a meeting, it’s a workflow. When the Senior Merchandiser approves, the whole planning cascade updates — nobody re-applies the new plan by hand in three separate systems.
So what
Outcome: Governance and speed together. There is a formal gate before the plan changes — and once through it, execution is immediate and consistent across the team.
Scene 5b · Homepage — Updated KPIs for the Approved Plan
Setup
Loop back to the connected planning homepage from Scene 1a. The approved scenario is now the live plan.
What to show
  • Every KPI now reflects the approved scenario: flare coverage improved, skinny markdown active, OTB rebalanced, supplier collaboration initiated with the compliance condition attached
  • Before and after in a single view — the scenario that was a branch two hours ago is the operating plan
  • Pre-season outlook already shows the in-season changes carried forward
Aha moment
Call out: The homepage wasn’t refreshed or rebuilt. It’s a live application over live computation — the plan changed, so the KPIs changed. The planner is looking at exactly what their MD will see this afternoon.
So what
Outcome: One version of the plan, visible to everyone, the moment it is approved. No “which deck is current?”

Section 6 — Pre-Season Propagation (the payoff)

Persona: Category Planner (return)
Scene 6a · Pre-Season Update — Changes Flow Forward
Setup
The closing scene is the payoff of a connected planning model: the in-season trend response doesn’t just fix Q2 — it informs pre-season planning for Q3, Q4 and Q1 next year without anyone rebuilding anything.
What to show
  • Pre-season planning view: the 20% skinny-to-flare reallocation has already propagated into the pre-season option plan — updated slot allocation for future seasons, no manual work
  • Attribute-level trend forecast: “What is the projected denim silhouette mix — skinny, straight, flare, wide-leg — for Q1 next year?” — computed from historical sell-through, segmentation trends and third-party fashion trend indices from the Marketplace
  • The planner reviews and approves the propagated changes, triggering a notification to the design team that line-plan slot allocation has moved and placeholder briefs need adjusting
  • The supplier picture travels forward too: the vendors that met the compliance bar under chase pressure are the ones now carried into the pre-season sourcing plan — the emergency decision has improved the standing supply base rather than quietly degrading it
Aha moment
Call out: This is the “black hole” solved. Q3, Q4 and pre-season plans have historically sat untouched while in-season actuals diverged from them. Here the in-season learning updates the forward plan automatically — the planner reviews and approves, rather than manually rebuilding.
So what
Outcome: Calla’s pre-season buying decisions for Q1 next year are informed by what is actually trending in Q2 this year — not by what was trending in Q2 last year, which is how most retailers plan. That is the competitive advantage.
